IN MEMORIAM
Vijaya Mehta
By Bhawana Somaaya
I was introduced to Vijaya Bai by Anupam Kher when he was shooting for Rao Saheb. Vijaya Mehta directed the film and played an important role in it. Anupam was fascinated by Bai and narrated delightful anecdotes about her unique style of working both on and off the sets. Those days, he was part of a creative committee where Vijaya Mehta was the chairperson, and he spent all his time minutely observing her.

Bharathiraja
By Subha J Rao
Bharathiraja is rightly credited with taking Tamil cinema out of studios to showcase village vistas. Along with the landscape, the storyscape changed too — the characters were more rooted and real, social issues such as caste, class divide and female infanticide were spoken about, and intense emotions drove relationships, both love and hate.

Bhagyaraj
By Subha J Rao
In Tamil Nadu of the eighties, if there was a director who tapped into the psyche of the female audience, and brought them to the theatres in droves, it was Bhagyaraj. His films had a hero, invariably him, but the one celebrated was the woman. Her joys, sorrows, fears, and more found space in his films.

BOOK OF THE MONTH
The Devil Finds Work
James Baldwin (English)
Recommended by Kshitij Rawat
“The Devil Finds Work” is a book-length essay about America, with cinema as its chosen language. James Baldwin uses films to interrogate myths the United States tells itself about race, desire, innocence, and power. His chief argument is that movies are never passive entertainment or escapism but repositories of a culture's aspirations and evasions. Baldwin is frank and even controversial — he writes, for instance, that David Lean's 1962 epic “Lawrence of Arabia” justified colonialism — but in a way that earns its conviction. An incredible book.

KNOW YOUR CRITIC
Suparna Sharma
A journalist since 1991, Suparna Sharma has written extensively on cinema, crime, corruption, insurgency and “all sorts of powers that disempower”. She won the 2026 Pulitzer Prize for Illustrated Reporting and Commentary, along with Anand RK and Natalie Obiko Pearson, for her Bloomberg piece ‘trAPPed’, about a neurologist held under digital arrest.
She’s written for, among others, Bloomberg, Al Jazeera, Rolling Stone, The Week, The Wire, Frontline, Indian Express, The Asian Age, and The Deccan Chronicle.
The first film that moved me:
I think it was “The Champ” (1979) by Franco Zeffirelli. They took us from school, and I was about nine years old then. When it comes to Indian films, it would be “Anand”. I’ve watched it more than a thousand times.
Over-rated masterpiece:
Sanjay Leela Bhansali’s Ranveer Singh “trilogy” — “Goliyon Ki Raasleela Ram-Leela”, “Bajirao Mastani”, and “Padmaavat”. They were operatic, lavish, gorgeous, made on a massive scale, and were an ode to symmetrical beauty. That left me totally cold, despite the fact that he is one of the finest directors in India. No one understands cinematic language the way he does, but in these films I felt he was operating like a very fastidious art director, who was particular even about the pleats of ghagras. I especially had an issue with the climax of “Padmaavat” for its beautification of violence.
Film you return to:
“Anand” and “Pakeezah”. I keep returning to Meena Kumari’s performance and “Pakeezah” itself, rewinding it scene by scene.
Film I would recommend:
M. S. Sathyu’s “Garm Hawa” (1974). Still a very relevant film — that seems to have been forgotten in popular memory — with some great writing, direction, and performances. That scene of Balraj Sahni asking for money and walking down the stairs continues to make me collapse emotionally.
