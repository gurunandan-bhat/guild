For an entire generation, the clash of arrows between Ram, Ravan and their respective armies formed the highlight of Ramanand Sagar's Ramayan. Every shot fired off their respective quiver resulted in divine friction pushing up sales of chintzy bow and arrow toy sets in shops and streets. But the moral fibre and human resolve at work in the age-old Hindu mythology of good winning over evil has a lot more complexity to offer than mere thrills. From poet Rabindranath Tagore to painter Raja Ravi Varma, it has inspired evaluation and expression from the greatest minds. How one construes Ramayana's idealism is telling of one's worldview -- back when dharma meant duty not religion. Two episodes of Shyam Benegal's Bharat Ek Khoj based on playwright Bhasa's interpretation capture the essence of the Ramayana's spiritual consciousness better than any of its sanctity-occupied counterparts. Over the years though, the Ramayana's Hindu identity and safeguarding has taken precedence over lessons to be learned from its holy text. One senses this influence in the timing and telling of Nitesh Tiwari's lavishly scaled drama. Abundant lotus sightings and motifs in Ayodhya's depiction play on the optics fitting in a reigning regime's political fetish if not formula.
